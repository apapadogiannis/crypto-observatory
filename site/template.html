What return should a cryptoasset's holders require?

The return an investor should demand before holding each asset, given the risks it carries. It is a hurdle rate from one published equation, not a forecast of what the asset will deliver.

By Agisilaos Papadogiannis · Method: Bitcoin's Required Return (working paper) · code · data

What each required return is made of

Each bar adds up to the required return, in percent a year. The thin line is the band: the range the answer takes when risk aversion and the holder's weight are set low or high. Below the dotted line, gold and three equity series are shown for scale.

risk-free ratemarket riskconcentration riskmortality riskband
Risk-free rateWhat a ten-year US Treasury pays. Every investment has to beat it.
Market riskThe asset falls when stocks fall. The more it moves with stocks (its beta), the more holders must be paid.
Concentration riskCrypto owners keep a large share of their wealth in a volatile asset, and cannot diversify that away. Stocks have none of it.
Mortality riskCryptoassets die. The pay for that is measured from how often top-twenty assets have died.

The price of the return: volatility

A higher required return is pay for more risk. The Sharpe ratio says how much: the return over the risk-free rate for each point of volatility. Volatility also takes a share of the return itself, about half the variance each year, so a volatile asset compounds at much less than its average return. Gold and three equity series are priced the same way for scale.

  • How the others are priced. The equity series by the CAPM: the risk-free rate plus beta times the 4% equity premium, with volatility measured since 2012. Gold by the same equation as the cryptoassets, as in the paper: a beta near zero, a holder with a few percent of wealth in it, and almost no risk of dying. Its Sharpe ratio is near zero.
  • Sharpe ratio, compound. The same ratio on the compound return. It is descriptive: the measure is defined on average returns.

The inputs behind each cryptoasset

    How the required returns have moved

    The midpoint of each asset's band at every month-end since it has been priced, percent a year. A required return falls when the asset becomes less volatile, moves less with stocks, or when more diversified investors hold it.

    How to read this page

    • A hurdle, not a forecast. A required return is what holders should ask for given the risk. An asset can deliver more or less for years.
    • Quote the figure with its band. Risk aversion and the holder's weight are not known exactly, so the answer is a range. The band is a range of inputs, not a statistical confidence interval.
    • Average and compound are different things. The required return is an average yearly return. A volatile asset compounds at much less: about half its variance is lost each year. The table gives both.
    • Only Bitcoin is tested. Bitcoin's requirement is set against what it delivered since 2012, under Bitcoin. The other assets are priced with the same equation and have too short a record to test.
    • A low figure is not a safety rating. An asset that has moved little with stocks gets a small market term. That says nothing about who controls it or how it is run.

    The band prices three holders. The lower edge is the average owner when fund and treasury investors are counted, with the low inputs. The upper edge is the larger of two candidates: the average surveyed owner with the high inputs, or the early holder, who was all-in while few people owned Bitcoin.     Whose risk the concentration term prices

    The concentration term is risk aversion × variance × the share of the holder's wealth that moves with Bitcoin. Variance comes from prices. The share cannot be read from prices: it depends on who holds Bitcoin, and it is measured from household surveys and company filings. The band prices three holders, and this tab shows where each weight comes from.

    The surveyed owner: % at every date

    US households that owned crypto in 2021 kept 19% of their financial assets in it on average, and the 2025 wave of the same survey reports similar shares (Weber, Candia, Coibion and Gorodnichenko 2026). Between the two waves Bitcoin's price rose 2.3 times. An owner who had simply held would have gone from 19% to about 35%. The surveys do not show that, so the page uses 19% in every month and does not move it with the price.

    Two national surveys ask owners what their holdings are worth, wave after wave. Each line below is a multiple of its value in the first wave. The price multiplies; the holding per owner stays close to ×1. A higher market value shows up as more owners, not as more per owner.

    The lower edge: adding fund and treasury investors

    Someone who owns a spot ETF, or shares of a company that holds Bitcoin, is not a "crypto owner" in a household survey, yet carries Bitcoin's risk. These investors are diversified: they are taken to keep % of their wealth in Bitcoin. Counting their wealth beside the surveyed owners' gives a lower average weight, the one behind the lower edge of the band.

    The resulting weight

    percent of wealth, average owner counting fund and treasury investors

    This weight does not move with the price. It falls only as funds and companies hold more of the supply, which is how the required return falls as more diversified investors hold Bitcoin. The holdings are from the Grayscale trust's filings to 2023, US spot ETFs from 2024, and public companies' reported holdings.

    The early holder

    While few people owned Bitcoin, the coins sat with holders for whom it was nearly everything they owned. The band gives that holder a weight of 100% as long as ownership is no higher than in the 2018 survey (% of US adults), and lets the weight fall in proportion as ownership grows: weight = % ÷ ownership. With log utility (γ = 1), that holder's required return is the risk-free rate plus the weight times variance. It sets the upper edge of the band whenever it is the higher of the two.

    Between surveys ownership grows at a constant rate. Before the first survey it follows the number of funded Bitcoin addresses.

    What is uncertain, and what changed

    • 19% is an average in which every owner counts once. The median owner held about 8%. What prices the asset is the share of owners' combined wealth, which no survey reports and which is lower if wealthier owners hold smaller shares. With the weight at 12% or at 8% the paper's required return for 2026 is one to two points lower (16.9% and 16.1% against 18.1%).
    • The portfolio share is observed in one survey. The 19% is from US households in 2021, reported as similar in 2025. The British and Canadian surveys report the value of holdings, not shares of wealth. No survey of shares exists before 2021, so earlier months use the same 19%.
    • Household surveys miss some holders. The largest holders, funds, and holders outside the surveyed countries are not in them. Funds and public companies are added as described above; the others are not.
    • Risk aversion is bounded, not measured. 1 at the lower edge and for the early holder, 2.8 at the upper edge. The weight and risk aversion together set most of the band's width.
    • What changed in November 2026. Earlier versions of this page let the surveyed owner's weight follow Bitcoin's market value, so it rose at every peak (80% at the end of 2017, 34% in mid-2025). That is not what the surveys show. With the weight held at 19% the required return of recent months is about one point lower.

    The same test as a regression

    percent a year

    The standard test of a pricing model regresses the asset's return over the risk-free rate on the equity market's and asks whether the intercept, alpha, is zero. For the MCM equation the concentration and mortality terms are subtracted from Bitcoin's return first. Here the slope is fitted by the regression; in the table above the beta is the rolling one known at each month-end, which is why the two differ slightly.

    Why delivered differed from required

    Read each period in two moves. First, the gap. The blue bar is the premium holders required over the risk-free rate, and the circle is what Bitcoin delivered. The black line between them is the gap: delivered minus required. Then, where the gap came from. Part of it came with the equity market: equities paid more, or less, than the 4% a year the equation assumes, and Bitcoin moves with them in proportion to its beta. The rest is left unexplained by the equation: it is the part specific to Bitcoin. Percent a year.

    percent a year over the risk-free rate

    Gap =
    Unexplained = delivered − required − beta × (equity market return − risk-free rate − 4%). Put another way, it is what Bitcoin delivered beyond the pay for all three risks, with the market part counted at what equities actually paid instead of the assumed 4%: delivered − beta × (equities' actual return over the risk-free rate) − concentration − mortality.

    required premiumdeliveredgap: delivered minus requiredof the gap: came with the equity marketof the gap: left unexplained, specific to Bitcoin

    The gap is the paper's monthly test. The unexplained return is the quantity behind the paper's "share explained", and its regression alpha is within a point or two of it (both under Tests). Showing one inside the other is the observatory's own reading and involves no new estimate. Two cautions. The split depends on the 4% equity premium, which is a convention: with a higher premium, more of the gap would count as required and less as coming with the equity market. And the equity part is only as firm as the beta: where the correlation with equities is low, as in the early periods, the beta is poorly measured and so is the split.

    Does the assumed equity premium matter?

    The market term is beta times the equity premium, which the paper holds at 4% a year. The premium that stock prices imply is not constant, so this section reruns everything with it, month by month, and with other fixed values. The required return moves by about beta times the change in the premium. The tests of fit hardly move, because the unexplained return is computed from what equities actually returned, not from the premium assumed.

    The MCM equation

    Every required return on this site comes from one equation. It is the CAPM plus the two risks the CAPM assumes away: that the holder has a lot of her wealth in one asset, and that the asset can die.

    By Agisilaos Papadogiannis · Method: Bitcoin's Required Return (working paper) · code · data

    The equation

    required return = risk-free rate + β × equity premium + γ × σ² × w + λ × (1 − δ)

    what a safe bond paysMarket riskConcentration riskMortality risk

    It answers one question: what return must holders expect, a year, to be willing to keep the asset, given the risks they carry? It is a required return at a date and for a holder. It is not a forecast of what the asset will do. Section 2 of the paper derives it.

    Build a required return

    Pick a case, then move any slider. The cases use this site's own data for .

    Risk-free rate

    What the holder earns with no risk at all: the yield on a ten-year Treasury bond. Everything else is paid on top of it.

    Market risk β × equity premium

    The asset falls when equities fall, and that cannot be diversified away. Beta (β) says by how much: 1 means it moves one for one with the equity market. Equities are taken to pay 4% a year over the risk-free rate, a convention; the Tests tab shows what changes with other values. This term alone is the CAPM.

    Concentration risk γ × σ² × w

    The CAPM assumes every holder is diversified. Bitcoin's holders are not: it is a large share of their wealth, so they carry its own ups and downs and must be paid for it. The pay grows with the share held (w), with the asset's variance (σ², volatility squared), and with how much the holder dislikes risk (γ, about 1 to 3 for households).

    Mortality risk λ × (1 − δ)

    The CAPM also assumes the asset cannot die. Cryptoassets do. The holder must be paid the loss she can expect: the chance the asset dies in a year (λ) times the share of her money lost if it does (1 − δ, where δ is what she recovers). The census of deaths measures both.

    required return a year

    From one holder to the band

    • One holder here, a range on the Bitcoin page. Risk aversion and the holder's weight are not measured exactly, so the Bitcoin page shows a band. Its lower edge is the holder through a fund and its upper edge the surveyed owner, the two Bitcoin cases above, with the market term taken 15% lower and 15% higher. Through 2018 the early holder set the upper edge. The Required return tab shows this month by month.
    • Each term rests on an established result. The market term is the CAPM. The concentration term is the standard portfolio-choice condition (Merton 1969), read as a price when only some investors hold the asset (Merton 1987). The mortality term is the usual compensation for a jump: probability times loss (Merton 1976).
    • It reduces to the CAPM. When the asset cannot die and holders are diversified, the last two terms vanish. That is the stock index fund case above.
    • It is tested. The Tests tab sets the equation against what Bitcoin delivered, month by month since 2012.

    The census of cryptoasset deaths

    How often cryptoassets die, whether surviving makes them safer, and what protects them. The census is where the mortality term of the required return comes from.

    By Agisilaos Papadogiannis · Method: Bitcoin's Required Return (working paper) · code · data

    Does surviving make an asset safer?

    The popular "Lindy" idea says the longer an asset has lived, the longer it will live, which would make the death rate fall like one over age: the dashed line. The data reject it. The death rate rises over the first years of an asset's life and stays high. Deaths per hundred assets a year, by age; whiskers are 95% intervals, and the number of deaths in each group is at the foot of its bar.

    The dashed line is k / age drawn through the bar for ages 2 to 3 years. The estimate of b comes from a regression of monthly deaths on the logarithm of age.

    What counts as a death, and how it is priced

    • Three ways to die. The data feed ends more than 180 days before the census date. Or the price falls below 1% of its peak and does not recover. Or trading dries up: three-month volume below 1% of its peak and under $250,000 a day, with no recovery.
    • What is not a death. A change of ticker, a redenomination or a merger, where holders received the successor token. Stablecoins, wrapped tokens and tokenized stocks are left out of the census.
    • Sudden collapses are entered by hand at their public dates.
    • From death rate to required return. A holder must be paid the loss she can expect: the chance the asset dies in a year, times the share of her money lost if it does. Read low, that is sudden collapses only, 0.30% a year at a 95% loss, or 0.3 points. Read high, it is every death of a top-twenty asset counted as a total loss, 3.5 points. Bitcoin's required return uses the range between them, 1.9 points at the midpoint.
    • One census, not yet a monthly one.

    What this site does not claim

    • Nothing here is a forecast. A required return is what holders should ask for given the risk, not what the asset will deliver.
    • The unexplained return is not a supply and demand measure. It is what the equation leaves over.
    • Repricing is not shown as a separate part.
    • Short windows say little. The tests are informative over years, not months.
