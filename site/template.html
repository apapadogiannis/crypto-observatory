Bitcoin: required and delivered return

What Bitcoin's holders should require for the risks they carry, set against what Bitcoin delivered. Built on the MCM equation: the risk-free rate plus market, concentration and mortality risk.

Why delivered differed from required

Read each period in two moves. First, the gap. The blue bar is the premium holders required over the risk-free rate, and the circle is what Bitcoin delivered. The black line between them is the gap: delivered minus required. Then, where the gap came from. Part of it came with the equity market: equities paid more, or less, than the 4% a year the equation assumes, and Bitcoin moves with them in proportion to its beta. The rest is alpha, the part specific to Bitcoin. Percent a year.

percent a year over the risk-free rate

Alpha = delivered − required − beta × (equity market return − risk-free rate − 4%). It is how far Bitcoin ran ahead of, or behind, what its risks and the equity market's own run account for.

The gap is the paper's monthly test, and alpha is the alpha of its regression test (both under Tests). Showing one inside the other is the observatory's own reading and involves no new estimate. The split depends on the 4% equity premium, which is a convention: with a higher premium, more of the gap would count as required and less as coming with the equity market.

How the band is built

The band prices three holders. The lower edge is the average owner when fund and treasury investors are counted, with the low inputs. The upper edge is the larger of two candidates: the average surveyed owner with the high inputs, or the early holder, who was all-in while few people owned Bitcoin. Each candidate is solid while it sets the upper edge and dashed while it does not, and the strip along the top names the holder who sets it. The midpoint is the simple average of the two edges; it is the required return used in the tests. Percent a year, log scale.

Concentration risk risk aversion (γ) × variance (σ²) × holder's weight (w)

Bitcoin's holders keep a large share of their wealth in one volatile asset and must be paid for it. Three inputs set the term. Variance and the holder's weight move through time and are charted below. Risk aversion is held constant and is bounded, not measured: 1 at the lower edge and 2.8 at the upper edge.

Mortality risk probability of death a year (λ) × share lost on death (1 − δ)

A holder must be paid the loss she can expect from the asset dying: the chance it dies in a year, times the share of her money she loses if it does. Both come from the census of cryptoasset deaths, read two ways. The term is the same at every date, because the census shows no fall in the death rate with age.

0.30% × 95% ≈ 0.3 ptslow reading: sudden collapses only (LUNA, UST, FTT), with 95% lost.
3.5% × 100% = 3.5 ptshigh reading: every death of a top-twenty asset, slow fades included, counted as a total loss.
(0.3 + 3.5) ÷ 2 = 1.9 ptsmidpoint, the figure in the required return.

What this page does not claim

  • Alpha is not a supply and demand measure. It is what the equation leaves over. In the data to May 2026, against monthly issuance, net exchange flows and changes in fund holdings together, it shows no reliable link (R² 0.19, no coefficient with a t-statistic above 1.7).
  • Repricing is not shown as a separate part. The month-to-month change in the required return moves with the return itself (correlation 0.90 in the data to May 2026), because volatility and the holder's weight are computed from the price.
  • Short windows say little. Month by month, the required return and the next month's return are uncorrelated. The tests are informative over years, not months.
  • Nothing here is a forecast. A required return is what holders should ask for given the risk, not what the asset will deliver.

Method and inputs: A. Papadogiannis (2026), "Bitcoin's Required Return: Pricing a New Asset Beside Equities and Gold", working paper. Paper and replication package: github.com/apapadogiannis/bitcoin-required-return. Required returns are dated at the month-end they are set; returns at the month they are earned. The alphas here are average unexplained returns with the rolling beta; the regression intercepts in the paper are 37 and 1 points a year for the MCM equation (whole sample, and since 2018).
