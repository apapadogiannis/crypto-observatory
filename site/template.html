Bitcoin: required and delivered return

What Bitcoin's holders should require for the risks they carry, set against what Bitcoin delivered. Built on the MCM equation: the risk-free rate plus market, concentration and mortality risk.

Why delivered differed from required

Read each period in two moves. First, the gap. The blue bar is the premium holders required over the risk-free rate, and the circle is what Bitcoin delivered. The black line between them is the gap: delivered minus required. Then, where the gap came from. Part of it came with the equity market: equities paid more, or less, than the 4% a year the equation assumes, and Bitcoin moves with them in proportion to its beta. The rest is left unexplained by the equation: it is the part specific to Bitcoin. Percent a year.

percent a year over the risk-free rate

Unexplained = delivered − required − beta × (equity market return − risk-free rate − 4%). Put another way, it is what Bitcoin delivered beyond the pay for all three risks, with the market part counted at what equities actually paid instead of the assumed 4%: delivered − beta × (equities' actual return over the risk-free rate) − concentration − mortality.

The gap is the paper's monthly test. The unexplained return is the quantity behind the paper's "share explained", and its regression alpha is within a point or two of it (both under Tests). Showing one inside the other is the observatory's own reading and involves no new estimate. Two cautions. The split depends on the 4% equity premium, which is a convention: with a higher premium, more of the gap would count as required and less as coming with the equity market. And the equity part is only as firm as the beta: where the correlation with equities is low, as in the early periods, the beta is poorly measured and so is the split.

How the band is built

The band prices three holders. The lower edge is the average owner when fund and treasury investors are counted, with the low inputs. The upper edge is the larger of two candidates: the average surveyed owner with the high inputs, or the early holder, who was all-in while few people owned Bitcoin. Each candidate is solid while it sets the upper edge and dashed while it does not, and the strip along the top names the holder who sets it. The midpoint is the simple average of the two edges; it is the required return used in the tests. Percent a year, log scale.

Mortality risk probability of death a year (λ) × share lost on death (1 − δ)

A holder must be paid the loss she can expect from the asset dying: the chance it dies in a year, times the share of her money she loses if it does. Both come from the census of cryptoasset deaths, read two ways. The term is the same at every date, because the census shows no fall in the death rate with age.

The same test as a regression

percent a year

The standard test of a pricing model regresses the asset's return over the risk-free rate on the equity market's and asks whether the intercept, alpha, is zero. For the MCM equation the concentration and mortality terms are subtracted from Bitcoin's return first. Here the slope is fitted by the regression; in the table above the beta is the rolling one known at each month-end, which is why the two differ slightly.

  • Average and compound. The required return is an average across years. A position that is bought and never rebalanced grows at the compound rate, which is lower by the drag: half the variance. For a volatile asset the drag is large.
  • While it survives. Bitcoin's figures are what holders require in the years the asset lives.
  • How each column is measured. Bitcoin's beta and volatility are the ones used in its required return, with recent months weighted more. The Sharpe ratio on the compound return is descriptive: the measure is defined on average returns.
  • Which series. The paper sets Bitcoin beside the S&P 500, a global index and the Russell 2000. This page uses the closest series it can download on a schedule, from the Kenneth R. French data library: the US stock market, developed markets, and the smallest 30% of US companies by size. Ether, gold and the Nasdaq-100, which the paper also shows, are not here yet.

What this page does not claim

  • The unexplained return is not a supply and demand measure. It is what the equation leaves over.
  • Repricing is not shown as a separate part.
  • Short windows say little. The tests are informative over years, not months.
  • Nothing here is a forecast. A required return is what holders should ask for given the risk, not what the asset will deliver.

The census of cryptoasset deaths

How often cryptoassets die, whether surviving makes them safer, and what protects them. The census is where the mortality term of the required return comes from.

Does surviving make an asset safer?

The popular "Lindy" idea says the longer an asset has lived, the longer it will live, which would make the death rate fall like one over age: the dashed line. The data reject it. The death rate rises over the first years of an asset's life and stays high. Deaths per hundred assets a year, by age; whiskers are 95% intervals, and the number of deaths in each group is at the foot of its bar.

What counts as a death, and how it is priced

  • Three ways to die. The data feed ends more than 180 days before the census date. Or the price falls below 1% of its peak and does not recover. Or trading dries up: three-month volume below 1% of its peak and under $250,000 a day, with no recovery.
  • What is not a death. A change of ticker, a redenomination or a merger, where holders received the successor token. Stablecoins, wrapped tokens and tokenized stocks are left out of the census.
  • Sudden collapses are entered by hand at their public dates.
  • From death rate to required return. A holder must be paid the loss she can expect: the chance the asset dies in a year, times the share of her money lost if it does. Read low, that is sudden collapses only, 0.30% a year at a 95% loss, or 0.3 points. Read high, it is every death of a top-twenty asset counted as a total loss, 3.5 points. Bitcoin's required return uses the range between them, 1.9 points at the midpoint.
  • One census, not yet a monthly one.
